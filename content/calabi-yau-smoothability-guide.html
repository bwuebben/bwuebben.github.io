How a polygon can rule out a smoothing

Start at an ordinary double point, or node. Its local equation is xy − zw = 0. Replace zero by a small nonzero number and the singularity disappears. The resulting family is a smoothing. Its central fibre is singular and its nearby fibres are smooth. In a toric threefold, a lattice polygon can encode the entire neighbourhood of a singular point. The unit square gives the node. Other polygons reveal two quite different ways a search for a smoothing can fail.

In one projective example the only singularity is the cone over the first Hirzebruch surface. Its defining polygon admits an infinitesimal deformation, but the parameter satisfies t² = 0. There is no nearby value of t other than zero. A second example is subtler. The singularity actually moves along a smooth curve of parameters, and every fibre along that curve is still singular. The paper on nonsmoothable threefolds gives both constructions. Its polygon test detects a local obstruction in 39,175,536 of the 473,800,776 reflexive four-polytopes in the Kreuzer–Skarke scan, about 8.27%. Assuming the scanned list is complete and free of duplicates, this is a lower bound on the proportion whose generic hypersurface admits no smoothing.

One can also use the polytope to construct a smoothing. When the edges are primitive, a chosen face cone is smoothable and the edge dual to that face has an interior lattice point, three-term equations deform the ambient fourfold and smooth the hypersurface along that face. This construction accounts face by face for 3,774 of the 30,241 smoothings in the Batyrev–Kreuzer census. The others bring different faces into the same homology relation. The ambient deformation paper also gives a seven-vertex example with a single node that no global deformation can smooth.

The threefold with thirty smoothable singularities

The example in the opening comes from looking for mirror pairs. A reflexive polytope has a polar dual, and the two polytopes specify mirror families of hypersurfaces. Among the 590 polytopes for which both generic hypersurfaces have at most isolated singularities, a unique mirror pair contains the cone over the first Hirzebruch surface. That cone rules out a smoothing on one side. The classification of doubly isolated mirrors gives a complete local inventory on the other side. All twenty-six nodes and four del Pezzo cone points pass the local test.

What could prevent those thirty smoothings from fitting together? Resolve a node by replacing it with a projective line. For a threefold with only nodes, Friedman's criterion asks for coefficients, all nonzero, such that the weighted sum of the exceptional curve classes vanishes in the homology of the resolution. For instance, if two exceptional curves have the same nonzero class, their difference is a relation that uses them both. If one curve is independent of all the others, every relation misses it.

At a cone point the resolution introduces an entire surface, and curves on that surface can enter the relation too. On the smooth part of a Calabi–Yau threefold there is a holomorphic volume form. Its integral over a three-cycle is called a period. Whenever a combination of cycles bounds in the complement of the singularities, Stokes’ theorem forces the same combination of periods to vanish. This is the source of the vanishing-cycle obstruction. It applies even when a putative smoothing starts with zero first derivative. In the thirty-point example every possible choice of local smoothing components fails the global condition. Even a formal one-parameter family cannot smooth all thirty. One cone, two ways to smooth it

The degree-six del Pezzo surface is the projective plane blown up at three noncollinear points. The hexagon in the opening describes its cone. Add two lattice triangles and one obtains that hexagon. Add three suitably directed lattice segments and one obtains it again. Here adding shapes means taking all sums of one point from each shape. The first decomposition gives a line of local deformations, the second a plane. The two components meet at the original cone.

On the plane component there are three marked periods a, b, c with a + b + c = 0. These are integrals of the volume form over the three cycles of a nodal modification. The diagram shows real values of the first two periods. The third is then determined. Each line is where one of the three periods vanishes.

On a compact threefold the available triples lie in the space of global relations. A relation can therefore move a cone and still leave a node behind. The mixed smoothing criterion makes this distinction precise for projective threefolds with nodes and anticanonical cones over smooth del Pezzo surfaces of degrees five, six and seven or over a smooth quadric surface, under the stated crepant resolution hypotheses. The degree-five del Pezzo surface is itself nontoric. A full smoothing exists exactly when, for some choice of local components, a global relation avoids every local discriminant, the locus of singular fibres. One projective example has four smooth components in its deformation space and no smooth fibre on any of them.

The partial smoothing theorem asks which singularities can remain together. For nodes and degree-six cones, suppose the resolution is smooth and projective, small at the nodes and the vertex blow-up at the cones. Choose a component at each cone. The corresponding global base is smooth, and the zero coordinates of the period vectors describe exactly which singularities persist. In particular, if no first-order deformation on this base moves a given cone, the cone stays fixed throughout the family.

Two threefolds in that paper each start with thirty nodes and two degree-six cones. Choose the line at the first cone and the plane at the second. On one threefold the best possible outcome leaves a single residual node. On the other, the same local choices permit a smooth fibre. Compare the two sets of curve relations.

What the nearby fibres cannot tell us

Two partial resolutions of the same hypersurface give a way to see what is lost by drawing only the nearby fibres. Both have a smooth six-dimensional space of ordinary parameter values. Both have eight independent directions to first order. Call the two extra coordinates x and y. Each has square zero. Can they vary together over a base that retains their product?

One hexagon in common

x² = y² = xy = 0

The two global relations meet at a hexagon. Its equation kills the mixed product.

Disjoint sets of hexagons

x² = y² = 0,  xy ≠ 0

Each relation still kills a square. Their supports are disjoint, so the mixed product survives.

The local equations theorem recovers this multiplication. For nodes and exact degree-five and degree-six del Pezzo cones, take a smooth projective crepant resolution, small at the nodes and given by blowing up the cone vertices. Form the product of the local deformation spaces and impose the linear homology relations on it. Keeping the equations of this intersection gives the entire global deformation space near the original fibre, up to independent smooth parameters.

The link-period theorem extends this description to arbitrary isolated Gorenstein toric threefold singularities on a connected normal projective threefold with trivial dualizing sheaf and vanishing irregularity. A link is the boundary of a small neighbourhood of a singular point. Integrating the volume form over its three-cycles places the local deformation space, with its nilpotents, in the cohomology of the link. In these coordinates the global condition says that the dual link classes sum to zero on a projective log resolution.

In a projective example with two identical cone singularities, the link classes at the two points must be negatives of one another. A direction s survives with s² ≠ 0 and s³ = 0. It extends to second order and every attempt to extend it to third order fails. For a single isolated toric singularity the first failure can occur at any prescribed order from two onwards. Realizing all those orders on projective threefolds remains open. Read the compact cubic example.

Particles, charges and Higgs transitions

What becomes light at a conifold?

In 1989 Philip Candelas, Paul Green and Tristan Hübsch found examples in which topologically different Calabi–Yau families meet at finite distance in the space of compactifications. That made it plausible that a string vacuum could reach the conifold. A puzzle remained. As the three-sphere collapsed, the four-dimensional low-energy theory appeared singular. Finite distance between distinct Calabi–Yau manifolds.

Andrew Strominger’s 1995 explanation starts with a type IIB three-brane wrapped on the collapsing sphere. Its mass falls with the volume it wraps. To a four-dimensional observer it is a charged particle, and omitting it from the low-energy theory produces the apparent singularity. Brian Greene, David Morrison and Strominger then argued that condensation of several such light states could connect branches with different internal topologies. Which combinations can condense depends on the charges they carry. Massless black holes and conifolds in string theory · Black hole condensation and the unification of string vacua.

What charge does the surviving node carry?

The degree-six period plane lets us pose the charge question for a particular partial smoothing. Choose “On one line” and a node survives. Now view the threefold as a compactification of M-theory to five dimensions. The light particle here comes from a membrane wrapped on a shrinking curve. Its electric charge under a bulk photon is an intersection number between the curve and the divisor associated with the photon. One photon can couple to particles from several singular points. When charged fields acquire expectation values and break gauge symmetry, a process called Higgsing, the common photon ties those local choices together. Its charges are entries of the same matrix that governs simultaneous smoothing.

Return to the two models with thirty nodes and two degree-six cones. Take a partial smoothing in each that removes all thirty original nodes and leaves one node at the second cone. In the first model a surviving photon measures one unit of electric charge there. In the second, the charge is zero under every surviving bulk photon. A local observer sees the same nodal equation in both models. The compact geometry predicts which charge is measured on each partially smoothed threefold. Identifying these geometric branches with a prescribed microscopic Higgs vacuum at finite Planck mass is a further open question. Global constraints on Higgsing · The residual-charge calculation. Following a transition through the quantum theory

In the X9 example, two nodes and a cone over a degree-seven del Pezzo surface share a relation. The three local sectors can Higgs only together. Across the transition the Hodge numbers change from (6, 122) to (3, 123). What happens to the gauge fields that survive? Continuing eight periods across the transition gives the genus-zero prepotential, the function that determines their scalar and electromagnetic couplings. The continuation reproduces the couplings calculated on the smooth side. Bulk gauging and quantum couplings.

Magnetic objects raise a different question. A proposed bound state can split when the phases of its constituents align. Their phases can be followed using those same periods. On the resolved threefold, two isolated stable sheaves each contribute +1 to a Donaldson–Thomas count. The calculation constrains possible decays as the transition is approached. Which magnetic states survive at the endpoint is still open. Magnetic sheaves and stability walls.

What changes when gravity remains dynamical? On a face of the X9 Kähler cone where four curves shrink, the associated particles carry charges with one primitive relation. Under a regularity hypothesis on the effective supergravity, the Higgs branch near the transition has a transverse quotient singularity ℂ²/ℤ₄ even at finite Planck mass. Where the whole del Pezzo surface collapses, the metric completion of the geometric branch has the same local form. Its identification with the Higgs branch of the interacting five-dimensional theory coupled to gravity remains open. The Higgs branch at finite Planck mass.

The metric also detects the change of topology. On the original side of the X9 transition, take the partial resolution with four nodes and follow a fixed smoothing that moves each node to first order. For its polarized Ricci-flat metrics along a fixed ray, exactly three positive eigenvalues of the Laplacian on two-forms tend to zero. All three decay at an inverse-logarithmic rate, with different leading coefficients determined by the global geometry. Integral topology and small eigenvalues.

What happens to curve counts?

There is also an enumerative question. Curves that differ on the resolution by an exceptional curve can acquire the same class on the smoothing. For a projective conifold transition with smooth algebraic total space and torsion-free second homology at both ends, one can remove the exceptional contributions and add the terms that specialize to a given class. In a compact four-node example, eight nonzero contributions sum to the count of a line class calculated independently on the smooth side. Universal curve counting under conifold transitions.

The related topological-string papers ask what the variable in a local curve count measures physically. For a smooth positive-genus curve with two normal lines of equal degree, exchanging two circle actions on a Calabi–Yau fivefold turns the variable counting Euler characteristic into a weight for rotating a plane. The two descriptions give the same stable-pair index in every positive curve degree. The companion paper lets the line bundles vary and compares the first-order response of both indices to a change in the weight of the volume form. Morrison’s question on the mirror

Reid asked how far we can travel between Calabi–Yau families through singular threefolds. David Morrison asked what that journey looks like in a mirror. His proposal was that a conifold transition should run in reverse. As curves shrink to nodes on the original threefold, three-spheres shrink on the mirror. Smoothing the original should then correspond to resolving the mirror. Through the Looking Glass.

The mirror prediction has a numerical test. A collection of nodes on the mirror can impose fewer conditions than there are nodes. Morrison predicted how many independent conditions to expect from the number of independent exceptional curve classes on the original resolution. The mirror conifold theorem recovers that number and determines the integer coefficients as well. After marking and orienting the nodes, an integer combination of exceptional curves bounds on the original side exactly when the same combination of vanishing three-spheres bounds on the mirror. The proof constructs the bounding four-chains from tropical two-cycles. The comparison holds for the specified Batyrev families on connected components of the nodal locus reached by the construction near the maximally degenerate limit.

For the mirror family in the X9 example, four vanishing spheres satisfy a single relation with coefficients (1, −1, −1, 1) on the unique codimension-three component. On every other four-node component the spheres are independent and the codimension is four. Every member of the codimension-three component admits a projective small resolution. For a general member this resolution is birational to a mirror of the original smoothing. The four-node comparison · The reversed transition.

In the opening picture a two-sphere shrank and a three-sphere appeared. The four-node calculation tells us something we could never see in a picture of a single node. The relation that ties four curves together on one threefold can be read, coefficient for coefficient, from the four spheres on its mirror.
